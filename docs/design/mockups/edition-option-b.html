The Grand Budapest Hotel

BY Rod Machen2014.03.27FILM~4 MIN

My basic inclination is, like many, to like every new film from Wes Anderson. He had me at Bottle Rocket, and even the ones I haven't enjoyed as much — The Life Aquatic, The Darjeeling Limited — haven't pushed me away.

So I walked in predisposed to delight, which is a dangerous way to enter a theater. The danger here turns out to be irrelevant. This is Anderson at full bloom: a nested set of stories, a doomed concierge, and a lobby boy whose loyalty becomes the film's beating heart.

The symmetry is doing real work, not just decoration. Every frame is a vitrine, and inside each one Anderson arranges grief and farce with the same careful hand. It should feel airless. It doesn't.

The plot is a confection, but the feeling underneath is genuine loss — for a continent, a courtesy, a way of moving through the world.— from the review

By the time the frame stories close, one inside the next, you understand the whole elaborate machine exists to mourn something specific. That it stays hilarious on the way there is the trick only Anderson seems able to pull.

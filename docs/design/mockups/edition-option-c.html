The Grand Budapest Hotel

By Rod Machen · March 27, 2014

My basic inclination is, like many, to like every new film from Wes Anderson. He had me at Bottle Rocket, and even the ones I haven't enjoyed as much haven't pushed me away.

So I walked into The Grand Budapest Hotel predisposed to delight, which is a dangerous way to enter a theater. The danger here turns out to be irrelevant. This is Anderson at full bloom: a nested set of stories, a doomed concierge, and a lobby boy whose loyalty becomes the film's beating heart.

The symmetry is doing real work, not just decoration. Every frame is a vitrine, and inside each one Anderson arranges grief and farce with the same careful hand. It should feel airless. It doesn't.

The plot is a confection, but the feeling underneath is genuine loss — for a continent, a courtesy, a way of moving through the world.

From the review

By the time the frame stories close, one inside the next, you understand that the whole elaborate machine exists to mourn something specific. That it stays hilarious on the way there is the trick only Anderson seems able to pull.
